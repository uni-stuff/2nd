★Most likely to be asked

These are the things the slides put in bold, boxed, or repeated. If you learn nothing else, learn these.

  1. 3 filaments table. Actin = G-actin + ATP, 2 strands, polar, flexible. Microtubule = αβ-tubulin + GTP, 13 strands hollow tube, polar, stiffest. Intermediate = fibrous subunit, 8 tetramers rope, no polarity, no nucleotide, strongest.
  2. 3 phases of actin polymerisation: nucleation (lag) → elongation (growth) → steady state (equilibrium). Cc = critical concentration, kon·C = koff.
  3. C vs Cc: C > Cc grows, C = Cc same length, C < Cc shrinks.
  4. Plus end (barbed) grows up to 10× faster, hydrolysis slower, lower Cc. Minus end (pointed) slow, hydrolysis faster. Cc(minus) > Cc(plus) (written in red on the slide). Plus ends at the leading edge push the membrane forward.
  5. T form vs D form: ATP-actin grows, ADP-actin falls off. Hydrolysis is slow in free actin, fast once in the filament. Most free subunits are T form (ATP ≫ ADP in the cell).
  6. Requirements for actin polymerisation: monomer availability, filament nucleation, fibre elongation.
  7. Who does what: thymosin blocks, profilin speeds, Arp2/3 = branches, formin = parallel, tropomyosin = side stabiliser, CapZ = + cap, tropomodulin = − cap, gelsolin (needs Ca²⁺) and cofilin (ADP-actin) = cut.
  8. Microtubule polarity: α-tubulin exposed at the − end, β-tubulin at the + end. Nucleated by γ-tubulin ring complex at the MTOC / centrosome. Subunits added and lost only at the ends.
  9. Drugs: Taxol stabilises; colchicine and nocodazole block tubulin. Both kill dividing cells because microtubule dynamics is crucial for the spindle.
  10. Motors sorted by: filament, direction, cargo. Myosin on actin → + end. Kinesin on MT → + end (out). Dynein on MT → − end (in). Main job: transport and positioning of organelles.
  11. Myosin cycle: attached → released (ATP binds) → cocked (ATP hydrolysis) → force-generating (Pi release → power stroke).
  12. Muscle: myosin II generates force, heads walk toward actin + end, thick filament is bipolar. Actin + ends anchored at the Z disc by capping proteins. Titin is elastic. Ca²⁺ rise is transient. Two big ATP users: sliding and Ca²⁺ pumping.
  13. Actin functions (boxed on slide): cell shape and strength · locomotion: lamellipodia and filopodia · pinching the cell in two.
  14. Crawling = protrusion, attachment, traction. Contraction at the back must be coupled with de-adhesion at the rear. Locomotion requires an initial polarization.
  15. Rho family: Cdc42 → filopodia, Rac → lamellipodia (front), Rho → stress fibres and focal adhesions (back). External signals: chemotaxis (diffusible gradient) and non-diffusible cues on ECM or cells.
  16. Summary slide: to crawl, a cell must (1) make and keep polarity (2) protrude at the leading edge with new actin (3) adhere the new part to the substratum (4) use motor forces to bring the body forward.
L6The big picture

What is inside a cell

Cell membrane wall between inside and outside.
Cytoplasm jelly that holds the organelles.
DNA the instructions. Kept in the nucleus (control centre).
Endoplasmic reticulum passageway that moves materials.
Golgi receives proteins in little bags (vesicles) and sorts them.
Mitochondrion powerhouse, makes ATP.
Lysosome garbage collector full of enzymes.
Cytoskeleton keeps the cell's shape. (This is what L7 and L8 are about.)

Cell → tissue → organ → system

Cells stack into tissues, tissues into organs, organs into systems. There are 4 tissue types: epithelial (covers surfaces), muscle (fibres that contract), nervous (cells with projections that send electrical signals), connective (holds stuff together).

Connective tissue has 3 kinds: fibrous (loose tissue, padding under skin) · skeletal (bone, cartilage: cells in a hard matrix) · fluid (blood: cells in a liquid matrix).
More detail (slide wording)
  • Tissues are "multiple cell types arising from progenitor or stem cells".
  • "Not all tissues are renewal tissues, but most display a small degree of renewal."
  • "Each tissue has a specific cellular composition and microarchitecture."
  • Systems named: skeletal (structure, protection), muscular (support, movement), digestive, respiratory, nervous, circulatory (O₂, nutrients, waste), lymphatic (defence), integumentary (skin, nails, hair), endocrine (hormones), urinary (blood filtration), reproductive.

ECM = the scaffolding outside cells

ECM (extracellular matrix) is a net of protein fibres outside the cells: collagen fibres, elastic fibres, reticular fibres, plus "ground substance" filling the gaps. Fibroblasts are the cells that make it.

Two main functions of ECM: ① provide mechanical support to the cells ② regulate cell behaviour.
Two glue proteins (transmembrane adhesion proteins):
Cadherin = cell to cell.
Integrin = cell to ECM. Outside the cell it holds ECM protein; inside it holds actin through talin, vinculin, kindlin. Integrin has an α subunit and a β subunit.
  • ECM
  • fibroblast
  • ground substance
  • cadherin = cell-cell
  • integrin = cell-ECM
  • talin · vinculin · kindlin

Why a biomedical engineer cares

When you put cells on a material, the cell never touches the bare material. ECM proteins land on the material first, and the cell grabs those with integrins (making focal adhesions with talin, vinculin, FAK, Src inside).

Chain to remember: substrate (chemistry, mechanics, topography) → ECM proteins (how strongly they stick, how many, what shape) → integrins / focal adhesion → cell response (adhesion, proliferation, differentiation, phenotype expression).
More detail
  • Slide title: "ECM proteins mediate cell-material interactions."
  • Lab 1 is about exactly this: Part 1 protein adsorption on a sample, Part 2 hydrogel preparation (macro / micro / nanogels), Part 3 cell-material interaction under the microscope.
  • Cytoskeleton matters for two big jobs shown on the overview slide: cell migration and cell division.
L7The 3 filaments

Why cells need a skeleton

A cell has to be correctly shaped, physically robust, properly structured inside, and able to change shape to grow, divide and move. All of that comes from a system of protein ropes called the cytoskeleton.

3 main filament systems with "different mechanical properties, dynamics and biological roles": actin filaments, microtubules, intermediate filaments.

Three kinds of "rope" inside a cell

Actin (microfilament) Round G-actin beads, each holding one ATP. Beads join head-to-tail, so the filament has a direction (polar). Two strands wind round each other (right-handed helix). Flexible. Most of it sits at the cell edge (periphery). Jobs: cell shape, crawling, pinching the cell in two.
Microtubule Pairs of α-tubulin + β-tubulin (a heterodimer), each pair holding one GTP. Pairs stack head-to-tail into a strand (protofilament), 13 strands make a hollow tube. Stiffest and straightest. Grow out from the centre to the edge (cytoplasmic array). Jobs: roads for cargo, parking organelles, spindle in division.
Intermediate filament Long stringy subunits with a central α-helix. No nucleotide, no direction. Job: pure strength, cage around the DNA.
Why all three are stable AND dynamic: each is built of many small subunits held by many weak (non-covalent) bonds in several strands. Breaking the middle means breaking many bonds at once (thermally stable, mechanically strong). Taking one subunit off the end breaks only a few bonds (dynamic). So subunits come and go only at the ends.
More detail (slide wording)
  • Actin and tubulin both "catalyse the hydrolysis" of their nucleotide. The energy "enables the filaments to remodel rapidly".
  • Microtubules are "more complex structures than the actin filaments".
  • Along a microtubule "each subunit is bound extremely tightly to its neighbours" (longitudinal), and neighbouring protofilaments form "lateral contacts".
  • Intermediate filament subunits "are small and can diffuse rapidly in the cytosol (but not the filaments)".
  • Cytoskeletal structures "can change or persist according to need, lasting from less than a minute to the cell's lifetime". Examples: rearrangement during mitosis; a neutrophil reshaping itself within minutes when it detects a bacterium.
  • G-actin · ATP
  • αβ-tubulin heterodimer · GTP
  • polarity
  • 13 protofilaments
  • 8 tetramers
  • non-covalent bonds
L7How actin grows

3 phases: slow start, fast growth, then flat

1 Nucleation (lag phase): a few beads find each other and make a seed (the "nucleus"). The seed is held by many bead-to-bead contacts. This step is slow.
2 Elongation (growth phase): beads snap onto the ends quickly.
3 Steady state (equilibrium phase): beads joining = beads leaving. Length stays the same.

Cc (critical concentration) = the amount of free actin at which joining and leaving are equal. The slide formula: kon · C = koff. So Cc = koff / kon. C is the concentration of free actin subunits.
C > Cc → beads add at both ends before hydrolysis, elongation faster than dissociation → grows.
C = Cc → addition rate equals loss rate → constant length.
C < Cc → hydrolysis at both ends, elongation slower than dissociation → shrinks.
Why the cell cares: "Importance of the regulation of actin filament formation: control cell shape and movement."
  • nucleation / lag
  • elongation / growth
  • steady state / equilibrium
  • Cc
  • kon C = koff

Plus end grows, minus end shrinks

A free actin bead carries a tightly bound ATP. Once it is in the filament, the ATP gets split into ADP. Beads with ATP are called the T form and like to stay. Beads with ADP are the D form and like to leave.

The plus end (barbed end) adds beads up to 10× faster because the bead changes shape when it joins the polymer. Hydrolysis is slower there. The minus end (pointed end) grows slowly and hydrolyses faster. So the minus end needs more free actin just to hold still:

Cc(minus end) > Cc(plus end) (this line is in red on the slide). Elongation faster and hydrolysis slower at the plus end. Elongation slower and hydrolysis faster at the minus end.
Result: if a motile cell points its fast-growing plus ends toward the leading edge, it can push its plasma membrane forward.
More detail (slide wording)
  • "Actin catalyse ATP hydrolysis: very slowly in free actin subunits, accelerated when incorporated into filaments."
  • "Most soluble subunits are in the T form; free concentration of ATP ≫ ADP."
  • Filament half-life: how long one actin bead spends in a filament as it treadmills. Can be 30 min or 30 s.
  • Persistence length: the minimum length (a few tens of µm) at which random thermal wobbling is likely to bend the filament.
  • Accessory proteins cross-link and bundle filaments into large structures that are "much more rigid than individual actin filament".
  • barbed (+) / pointed (−)
  • T form / D form
  • treadmilling
  • half-life 30 s to 30 min
  • persistence length

Helper proteins: who does what

Requirements for actin polymerisation (3): actin monomer availability · filament nucleation · fibre elongation. Which filaments form where depends on the balance between stabilising and destabilising (sequestering) accessory proteins.
Thymosin (slows) grabs free beads so they can't join. Makes polymerisation "much less favourable".
Profilin (speeds) binds the bead on the face opposite the ATP site, leaving free the face that binds the + end. After the bead binds, it changes shape and profilin lets go. Profilin and thymosin compete for the same beads.
Arp2/3 complex (nucleator) brings beads together to make a seed on the side of an existing filament. Blocks the − end, lets the + end grow → branches (a web, used in lamellipodia).
Formin (nucleator) a dimer; each half holds one bead, so it captures 2 monomers to start a filament, then stays on the growing + end → straight parallel filaments (filopodia, stress fibres).
Tropomyosin (side binder) long protein lying along the filament, covers 6-7 beads. Stabilises but still allows growth.
CapZ / capping protein (+ end) reduces both growth and depolymerisation at the + end.
Tropomodulin (− end) binds the − end of actin already stabilised by tropomyosin (or pure actin). Strongly reduces both elongation and depolymerisation.
Gelsolin (severs) breaks a filament into many short pieces and caps the + end. Switched on by high cytosolic Ca²⁺.
Cofilin (severs) prefers ADP-actin, so it takes apart the older filaments.
Bundle / cross-link / attach: fimbrin and α-actinin tie filaments in parallel bundles. Filamin makes a gel network. Spectrin and ERM tie actin to the plasma membrane.
Two rules the slide spells out: nucleation "occurs primarily at the cell membrane". And "an actin filament that stops growing and is not stabilised in the cell will depolymerise rapidly".
L7Microtubules

They all start at the centrosome

The centrosome = MTOC (microtubule-organising centre). It is a blob of fibrous proteins organised by a pair of centrioles (short cylinders of modified microtubules), and it is rich in γ-tubulin. It sits free in the cytoplasm, next to the nucleus.

γ-tubulin ring complex (γ-TuRC): two accessory proteins bind γ-tubulin and make a spiral ring. That ring is the template that makes a microtubule with exactly 13 protofilaments. It stays stuck on the − end.
Polarity: α-tubulin is exposed at the − end, β-tubulin at the + end. All subunits in one protofilament point the same way.

Centrioles recruit the γ-rings via other proteins, but they are "not absolutely required for microtubule nucleation". Microtubules "continuously grow and shrink along the entire volume of the cell".

What this gives the cell: microtubules can find the centre of the cell and position organelles; and they play a big role in cell division (next lectures).
  • centrosome = MTOC
  • centrioles
  • γ-TuRC
  • 13 protofilaments
  • α at − end · β at + end

Why a tube is stable AND dynamic

Microtubules are the "stiffest and straighter structural elements, much more rigid than actin". Two kinds of contact hold them: longitudinal (subunit to subunit along a strand, extremely tight) and lateral (strand to strand). Addition and loss of subunits happen "exclusively at the ends".

Things that change microtubules

Drugs (bold on slide): Taxol binds microtubules and stabilises them (pushes toward polymerisation). Colchicine and nocodazole bind tubulin subunits (push toward depolymerisation). Both "preferentially kill dividing cells, since microtubule dynamics is crucial for correct function of the mitotic spindle". That is why they are cancer drugs.
Catastrophe factors (Kinesin-13) bind the microtubule ends and peel them apart → more depolymerisation, more "catastrophes".
XMAP215 binds tubulin dimers and delivers them to the + end → faster growth, catastrophes suppressed.
Stathmin binds two tubulin heterodimers at once so they can't be added to the end → less assembly.
Katanin severs; releases microtubules from the MTOC → rapid depolymerisation. Used at the spindle poles during mitosis.
MAPs (microtubule-associated proteins) hundreds of them. Tau and MAP2 bind along the sides and stabilise / bundle. Plectin links microtubules to intermediate filaments.
+TIPs (plus-end tracking proteins) stay on the growing + end and link it to other structures such as membranes.
  • Taxol = stabilise
  • colchicine · nocodazole = block
  • kinesin-13
  • XMAP215
  • stathmin
  • katanin
  • MAPs · +TIPs
L7Intermediate filaments

Built like a rope, no direction

Monomer: a long string with a conserved α-helical middle, NH₂ at one end and COOH at the other. Dimer: two monomers twist together the same way (coiled coil, 48 nm). Tetramer: two dimers lie side by side, staggered, pointing opposite directions. Because they point opposite ways, both ends of the tetramer look the same → no polarity. Filament: 8 tetramers (= protofilaments) pack side by side into a rope held by lateral hydrophobic contacts.

Remember the list: symmetrical, no structural polarity · no nucleotide binding site · no hydrolysis · can be disassembled rapidly when needed · line the inner face of the nuclear envelope (protective cage for DNA) · in the cytosol twisted into strong cables · higher resistance to stretching and bending than actin or microtubules · main function: provide mechanical strength.

Assembly / disassembly is "less understood" but is regulated by protein phosphorylation. The network is remodelled during cell division, migration and differentiation.

  • coiled-coil dimer
  • staggered tetramer
  • 8 protofilaments
  • no polarity
  • phosphorylation
L8Motor proteins

Actin's three jobs (slide 2 of L8)

① Determine the shape of the cell surface and provide strength.
② Necessary for cell locomotion. Form cell-surface projections (lamellipodia and filopodia). (This one is boxed on the slide.)
③ Drive pinching of one cell into two (cell division).

Walkers that carry cargo

A motor protein grabs a polarised filament and walks along it using energy from repeated cycles of ATP hydrolysis. Their main job: "organise membrane-enclosed organelles in appropriate locations in the cell".

Motors are classified by 3 things: ① which filament they bind (actin or microtubule) ② which direction they move (kinesins vs dyneins) ③ what they carry (membrane-enclosed organelles, or other cytoskeletal filaments).
Myosin walks on actin, toward the + end. Myosin II = muscle and non-muscle contraction. Myosin V = carries organelles along actin; different tails and light chains let each myosin pick its cargo. (Lever arm swings 30-40 nm.)
Kinesin walks on microtubules toward the + end = out to the periphery. Motor domain is at the N-terminus of the heavy chain. Links to an organelle via a light chain or adaptor protein.
Dynein walks on microtubules toward the − end = in to the centre. "The largest molecular motors and one of the fastest." Made of 1, 2 or 3 heavy chains (with the motor) plus many intermediate and light chains.
Polarised transport: microtubules sit with − ends at the centrosome and + ends at the edge. So kinesin moves vesicles toward the periphery, dynein toward the centre. "Major function of the cytoskeletal motors: transport and positioning of membrane-enclosed organelles."
More detail: dynein types
  • Cytoplasmic dyneins (homodimers of two heavy chains):
    Dynein 1: organelle and mRNA trafficking, positioning the centrosome and nucleus during migration, building the spindle in mitosis and meiosis.
    Dynein 2: only in organisms with cilia; carries material from tip to base of the cilium (intraflagellar transport).
  • Axonemal (ciliary) dyneins (monomers, heterodimers, heterotrimers with 1-3 motor heavy chains): fast sliding of microtubules that drives beating of cilia and flagella.
  • polarised filament
  • ATP hydrolysis cycles
  • kinesin + end
  • dynein − end
  • N-terminus motor domain

Myosin II shape

Two heads (N-terminus) with light chains at the neck or hinge, then a long coiled coil of two α-helices (about 150 nm) ending at the C-terminus. The tails of many myosin II stick together (tail-tail) to make a big bipolar thick filament with ~300 heads, heads pointing opposite ways at the two ends and a bare zone in the middle.

Actin-myosin complex = contractile structures that cross-link and slide actin filaments, in muscle AND non-muscle cells.
Switch-on: when MLCK (myosin light chain kinase) phosphorylates the light chains, the tail unfolds and the myosins self-assemble (active state). Not phosphorylated = folded, inactive.

How myosin takes one step (cross-bridge cycle)

1 Attached: a myosin head with no ATP is tightly bound to actin (rigor).
2 Released: ATP binds to the back of the head → small shape change in the actin-binding site → head lets go.
3 Cocked: ATP hydrolysis triggers the lever arm to move. ADP and Pi stay tightly bound.
4 Force-generating: head binds weakly to a new site → Pi is released → tight binding → the power stroke (force-generating shape change) → ADP leaves → back to 1.
Kinesin cycle (two heads): rear head is tightly bound with ATP; leading head is loosely bound with ADP. ADP dissociation + ATP hydrolysis = power stroke. ATP binding to the rear head flips a small peptide (the neck linker), pulling the rear head forward: it detaches, passes the partner head, and rebinds the next tubulin site. Hand over hand.
L8Muscle contraction

Sarcomere = one unit of muscle

A muscle cell (muscle fibre) is packed with myofibrils. Each myofibril is a chain of sarcomeres: "a highly organised array of parallel and partly overlapping thin (actin) and thick (myosin) filaments". Under the microscope you see dark bands (thick + thin overlap) and light bands (thin only), with a Z disc at each end and the M line in the middle.

How it contracts (slide bold): Myosin II generates the force. Each myosin head binds and hydrolyses ATP and uses the energy to walk toward the plus end of an actin filament. The thick filament is bipolar (heads at the two ends face opposite ways), so it slides the two oppositely oriented sets of actin toward the middle → the sarcomere shortens → muscle contracts.
Z disc the actin + ends are anchored here, "built from capping proteins" (CapZ). The caps stop depolymerisation and hold the filaments together. The − ends point to the middle and are capped by tropomodulin. Nebulin runs along the thin filament.
Titin big elastic molecule closely associated with the myosin filament; it changes length as the sarcomere contracts and relaxes (keeps myosin centred).
Other coat proteins cover the filaments and cap the ends "to avoid depolymerisation".
Ca²⁺ is the on switch. Muscle contraction depends on two processes that use a lot of ATP: filament sliding and Ca²⁺ pumping. Ca²⁺ is released by the sarcoplasmic reticulum; Ca²⁺ flooding the cytosol starts contraction of each myofibril. The troponin complex (I, C, T) and tropomyosin sit on the thin filament and, with Ca²⁺, move to let myosin bind. The increase in Ca²⁺ is transient: Ca²⁺-ATPase pumps rapidly pump it back into the SR membrane, concentration returns to resting level, myofibrils relax.

  • Z disc · M line
  • dark band · light band
  • bipolar thick filament
  • titin · nebulin
  • troponin I C T
  • Ca²⁺ transient
L8How cells crawl

Who crawls and why it matters

"Almost all cell locomotion occurs by crawling."

Nerve cells long-distance crawling builds the entire nervous system.
Macrophages and neutrophils crawl to sites of infection and eat invaders (innate immune response).
Osteoclasts dig channels in bone; osteoblasts fill them in (continuous bone remodelling).
Fibroblasts crawl through connective tissue, remodel it and repair damage.
Cancer cells crawl out of a tumour into blood or lymph vessels and come out elsewhere = metastasis.

First the cell needs a front and a back

"Cytoskeleton determines cellular organisation and polarity." It lets a cell tell top from bottom (apical vs basal, like gut cells with microvilli on top) or front from back, by building different structures with different molecules at each end.

Cell locomotion requires an initial polarization of the cell to set it off in a particular direction.

Three processes make this happen: dynamic assembly / disassembly of the polymers · regulation by polymer-associated proteins · motor proteins pulling on the polymers (tension).

Front vs back: Rho family switches

Polarity "depends on the local regulation of the actin cytoskeleton", switched on by outside cues through receptors, or by inside signals. The outside signals work through the Rho protein family: Cdc42, Rac, Rho.

Rac-GTP (front) → WASp family → Arp2/3 → branched actin web = lamellipodium. Also → PAK → blocks MLCK → less myosin activity, fewer stress fibres. Also filamin (web cross-linker).
Rho-GTP (back) → ROCK (Rho kinase) → MLC phosphorylated → more myosin; → LIM kinase → blocks cofilin; → formins → actin bundles. Result: more stress fibres → integrin clustering and focal adhesion formation.
Cdc42 → filopodia (thin spikes of parallel actin).
External signals, two types:
Chemotaxis = movement toward or away from a diffusible chemical (a gradient). Example on the slide: a neutrophil chasing a bacterium; at the front Rac dominates (polymerisation, protrusion), at the back Rho dominates (actin-myosin contraction), and the two inhibit each other.
Non-diffusible cues stuck to the ECM or to another cell's surface → activate receptors → more adhesion and directed actin polymerisation → steer the direction.
  • Cdc42 → filopodia
  • Rac → lamellipodia
  • Rho → stress fibres
  • WASp · Arp2/3
  • ROCK · MLCK
  • chemotaxis

Push, stick, pull

"Cell migration depends on the actin beneath the plasma membrane." Three activities that "may happen simultaneously or in a more independent way":

1 Protrusion the plasma membrane is pushed out at the front (while the back contracts). Actin polymerisation at the + ends pushes out the lamellipodium. The actin cortex is under tension.
2 Attachment the actin cytoskeleton connects across the plasma membrane to the substratum, through focal adhesions (which contain integrins).
3 Traction the bulk of the cytoplasm is drawn forward. Myosin II contracts at the back.
Two rules on the slide: "Protrusion of the membrane is followed by adhesion to the substratum at the front." And "Contraction must be coupled with de-adhesion at the rear of the cell." Coordinated movement = actin polymerisation + dynamic adhesions + myosin contraction.

4 ways actin is arranged

"Different types of protrusive structures depend on specialised accessory proteins." The slide shows 4 arrays in one cell:

Contractile bundle = stress fibre. Loose bundle (α-actinin), myosin fits between the filaments.
Gel-like network = cell cortex. Made by gel-forming proteins with actin-binding sites (filamin).
Dendritic (branched) network = lamellipodium. Nucleated by Arp2/3.
Tight parallel bundle = filopodium. Bundling proteins cross-link filaments into a parallel array, produced by formins.

The front keeps recycling actin

Four slide bullets, learn in order:
① Filament nucleation is localised at the leading edge; new actin grows there to push the membrane forward.
② Most depolymerisation happens well behind the leading edge.
③ Cofilin prefers ADP-actin. New T-form filaments resist cofilin. As filaments age and ATP is hydrolysed, cofilin can take the older filaments apart.
④ Delayed ATP hydrolysis → treadmilling in the lamellipodium.

Capping protein stops the older + ends; the freed monomers diffuse back to the leading edge and get used again.

Myosin II and microtubules in crawling, and the clutch

Roles of myosin II in migration (4): ① helps connect actin to the substratum through integrin adhesions ② actin polymerisation + myosin activity create tension at attachment sites, which matures them into focal adhesions ③ bipolar myosin II at the rear of the lamellipodium pulls actin filaments into a new orientation ④ sarcomere-like contraction prevents protrusion at the sides and pinches them in, gathering the cell as it moves.
Roles of microtubules in migration (3): ① organise persistent movement in one direction (the centrosome's position follows where protrusion happens) ② influence actin rearrangement and guide focal adhesion dynamics ③ transport cargo to and from focal adhesions, affecting their signalling and disassembly.
Strong interactions = clutch. "Strong interactions between the actin network and the focal adhesions are required to push the leading edge forward." Myosin force is transmitted to the ECM through the focal adhesions → traction → new actin polymerisation drives the leading edge forward.
Retrograde flow = when the actin-adhesion interactions are disengaged, polymerisation pressure at the front plus myosin contraction make the actin network slip backward instead of pushing the cell forward. Engaged = force goes into the ground and the edge advances.
Stretch a cell (external mechanical tension) → it assembles more stress fibres and focal adhesions and becomes more contractile.
Migration overall: "Driven primarily by actin polymerisation and myosin contractility. Interactions among filaments are required; microtubules and intermediate filaments also participate. Mechanical linkage to the nucleus is required."

Summary slide (learn word for word)

For a cell to crawl, it must:
① Generate and maintain an overall structural polarity (influenced by external cues).
② Coordinate protrusion at the leading edge (by assembly of new actin filaments).
③ Coordinate adhesion of the newly protruded part of the cell to the substratum.
④ Coordinate forces generated by molecular motors to bring the cell body forward.
✓Test yourself (tap to reveal)
Which filament has no polarity and no nucleotide?
Intermediate filaments. Tetramers are made of two dimers pointing opposite ways, so both ends are identical. No ATP/GTP binding site, no hydrolysis.
What are the subunits of actin, microtubules and intermediate filaments?
Actin: globular G-actin bound to ATP. Microtubule: αβ-tubulin heterodimer bound to GTP. IF: elongated fibrous subunit with a conserved α-helical domain.
How many protofilaments in each?
Actin 2 (helix), microtubule 13 (hollow tube), intermediate filament 8 tetramers (rope).
Name the 3 phases of actin polymerisation.
Nucleation (lag phase) → elongation (growth phase) → steady state (equilibrium phase).
What happens when C < Cc?
Hydrolysis at both ends; elongation is slower than dissociation; the filament shrinks. (C > Cc grows, C = Cc stays the same.)
Which end has the higher critical concentration, and why does it matter?
The minus (pointed) end: Cc(minus) > Cc(plus). Between the two values, subunits add at + and leave at − = treadmilling. Pointing + ends at the leading edge pushes the membrane forward.
T form vs D form?
T form = ATP-actin, grows more favourably. D form = ADP-actin, dissociates more favourably. Most soluble subunits are T form.
Three requirements for actin polymerisation?
Actin monomer availability, filament nucleation, fibre elongation.
Profilin vs thymosin?
Both bind free monomers and compete. Thymosin blocks assembly (slows). Profilin binds opposite the ATP site, exposes the + end binding face, speeds elongation.
Arp2/3 vs formin?
Arp2/3 nucleates on the side of a filament, caps the − end → branched web (lamellipodia). Formin is a dimer that nucleates and stays on the growing + end → straight parallel filaments (filopodia, stress fibres).
Which protein caps the plus end, which caps the minus end?
CapZ (capping protein) caps the plus end. Tropomodulin caps the minus end.
Gelsolin vs cofilin?
Both sever. Gelsolin is activated by high cytosolic Ca²⁺ and caps the + end. Cofilin binds ADP-actin, so it disassembles older filaments.
Which tubulin is at the plus end of a microtubule?
β-tubulin at the plus end, α-tubulin exposed at the minus end.
What is the γ-TuRC and where is it?
γ-tubulin ring complex: two accessory proteins bind γ-tubulin to make a spiral ring that templates a 13-protofilament microtubule. Found at the MTOC / centrosome, stays at the minus end.
Are centrioles required for microtubule nucleation?
No. They recruit γ-rings but are "not absolutely required".
Why do Taxol and colchicine kill dividing cells?
Taxol stabilises microtubules, colchicine/nocodazole block tubulin. Either way dynamics stop, and microtubule dynamics is crucial for the mitotic spindle.
Kinesin-13, XMAP215, stathmin, katanin: one line each.
Kinesin-13 = catastrophe factor, peels ends apart. XMAP215 = delivers dimers to + end, growth up. Stathmin = binds 2 dimers, blocks addition. Katanin = severs, frees MTs from the MTOC.
Main function of intermediate filaments, and how is their assembly regulated?
Mechanical strength (highest resistance to stretching and bending; cage around the nucleus). Regulated by protein phosphorylation.
Motor proteins are classified by which 3 things?
Filament they bind (actin or microtubule), direction they move (kinesin + / dynein −), molecules they carry.
Which motor goes to the cell periphery, which to the centre?
Kinesin → plus end → periphery. Dynein → minus end → centre. Myosin walks on actin toward the plus end.
Put the myosin cycle in order with the ATP events.
Attached (no ATP, tight) → Released (ATP binds) → Cocked (ATP hydrolysis moves lever arm, ADP+Pi bound) → Force-generating (weak bind new site, Pi released, tight bind, power stroke, ADP released).
What activates myosin II?
MLCK phosphorylates the light chains → tail unfolds → spontaneous self-assembly into filaments.
Why does a bipolar thick filament shorten a sarcomere?
Heads at the two ends point opposite ways. Each head walks toward the actin plus end (anchored at the Z disc), so the two sets of oppositely oriented actin are pulled toward the middle and the Z discs come closer.
Where are actin plus ends in a sarcomere and what holds them?
At the Z disc, anchored by capping proteins (CapZ). Minus ends point to the middle, capped by tropomodulin.
Two ATP-hungry processes in muscle contraction?
Filament sliding and Ca²⁺ pumping (Ca²⁺-ATPase pumps return Ca²⁺ to the sarcoplasmic reticulum, so the Ca²⁺ rise is transient).
What does titin do?
Elastic molecule associated with the myosin filament; changes length as the sarcomere contracts and relaxes.
Three activities of cell crawling?
Protrusion (membrane pushed out at the front, contraction at the back), attachment (actin connects through the membrane to the substratum), traction (cytoplasm drawn forward).
Cdc42, Rac, Rho: what does each make?
Cdc42 → filopodia. Rac → lamellipodia (via WASp → Arp2/3; lowers myosin via PAK). Rho → stress fibres and focal adhesions (via ROCK → more myosin; formins).
Define chemotaxis.
Movement of a cell toward or away from the source of a diffusible chemical (a chemical gradient).
Match: stress fibre, cell cortex, lamellipodium, filopodium → array type and builder.
Stress fibre = contractile bundle. Cortex = gel-like network (gel-forming proteins). Lamellipodium = dendritic network (Arp2/3). Filopodium = tight parallel bundle (bundling proteins, formins).
Why does cofilin only chew the back of the lamellipodium?
Cofilin prefers ADP-actin. New filaments at the front are T form (ATP) and resist it. Hydrolysis is delayed, so only older filaments behind the edge are disassembled → treadmilling.
What is retrograde flow?
When actin-adhesion interactions are disengaged, polymerisation pressure at the leading edge and myosin contraction make the actin network slip backward instead of pushing the cell forward.
What happens if you stretch a cell?
It assembles stress fibres and focal adhesions and becomes more contractile.
Four things a cell must do to crawl (summary slide)?
Generate and maintain polarity (external cues) · coordinate protrusion at the leading edge by new actin assembly · coordinate adhesion of the new part to the substratum · coordinate motor forces to bring the cell body forward.
Cadherin vs integrin?
Cadherin = cell-cell adhesion. Integrin = cell-ECM adhesion; binds ECM outside and actin inside via talin, vinculin, kindlin.
Two main functions of the ECM, and which cells make it?
Mechanical support for cells; regulate cell behaviour. Secreted by fibroblasts.
